## What's Different in Paid Search

Paid search ads operate in a competitive auction environment. Unlike content marketing — where
{{COMPANY}} avoids naming competitors entirely — paid search campaigns often **directly target
competitor keywords** (e.g., "{{COMPETITOR_D}} alternatives," "{{COMPETITOR_A}} vs {{COMPANY}}," "better than {{COMPETITOR_B}}").
In those contexts, naming competitors is **permitted and strategically appropriate**.

The distinction is intent:
- **Competitive bidding context**: Competitor names are fair game to establish contrast
- **General brand copy**: Never mention competitors — {{COMPANY}} stands on its own

---

## Competitor Mention Rules

### When competitor names ARE allowed

1. The ad group or campaign is explicitly targeting competitor keywords
   - Examples: "{{COMPETITOR_D}} alternatives," "[Competitor] pricing," "[Competitor] review," "[Competitor] vs"
2. The headline or description is drawing a direct comparison (we win on a specific dimension)
3. The user's optimization direction explicitly references competitor positioning

### When competitor names are NOT allowed

1. General campaign ads that aren't targeting competitor search terms
2. Any ad where the mention is defensive or fear-based ("unlike {{COMPETITOR_A}}, we won't lock you in")
3. Any mention that could read as endorsement, partnership, or confusion
4. When the comparison isn't specific — vague "better than the competition" framing

### Framing rules when naming a competitor

- **Always position {{COMPANY}} as the upgrade, not the escape.** "Switch to {{COMPANY}}" > "Tired of [Competitor]?"
- **Never disparage.** No "unlike [Competitor] who charges hidden fees." No specific FUD.
- **Claim only what's true.** Don't assert superiority on a dimension you can't back up.
- **Use comparison framing sparingly.** Best used in headline 1 or description 1 where context-setting matters most.

**Good examples** (character counts are for the rendered copy, not the token):
- `{{COMPANY}} vs {{COMPETITOR_A}} — Compare` (30 chars) — headline for a competitor comparison ad group
- `Used {{COMPETITOR_D}}? Try {{COMPANY}}` (28 chars) — switcher ad
- `Moving off {{COMPETITOR_A}}?` (22 chars) — question format, leaves room for a pinned second headline

**Bad examples:**
- `{{COMPETITOR_A}} is confusing. Try {{COMPANY}}.` (36 chars) — disparaging, no specific claim, and over the limit
- `Don't Get Locked in with {{COMPETITOR_C}}` (39 chars + fear-based)
- `Unlike {{COMPETITOR_D}}, we don't charge per page` — unverifiable specific claim about someone else's price book

## What Stays the Same in Paid Search

### Still prohibited in all contexts

| Category | Examples |
|----------|---------|
| Fear-based reliability (the cardinal sin) | "before your next outage," "what downtime costs you," "avoid SLA penalties," "outage risk," "angry customers," any downtime-cost math |
| Prevention claims | "never miss an alert again," "prevent incidents," "zero downtime." {{COMPANY}} shortens incidents, routes them, and helps teams learn from them — it does not prevent them, and Aaron will close the tab |
| Outcome uncertainty | "might," "could help you," "may reduce" — paid search copy must be confident |
| Corporate filler | "solution," "platform that enables," "streamline your," "leverage our," "all-in-one," "easy-to-use" |
| Unsourced availability numbers | Any uptime, delivery, or latency figure that does not come from the status page or the current SLA |
| Clickbait/misleading | Headline promises the landing page doesn't fulfill |

### Character limits are always hard

- Headlines: **≤30 characters** — no exceptions, no matter how good the copy sounds
- Descriptions: **≤90 characters** — count includes spaces and punctuation

### Tone stays {{COMPANY}}

Even in competitive ads, tone must stay A–B (dry-understated to lightly playful). Paid search
is not the place for aggressive salesmanship or combative framing. The most effective {{COMPANY}}
competitive ads are confident and specific, not combative.

## Decision Tree: Should This Ad Use a Competitor Name?

```
Is the campaign/ad group targeting competitor-branded keywords?
├── YES → Competitor names are allowed. Apply framing rules above.
└── NO
    └── Does the user's direction explicitly request competitor positioning?
        ├── YES → Competitor names allowed for that specific pass. Apply framing rules.
        └── NO → No competitor names. Use {{COMPANY}}-only framing.
```

## Paid Search Persona Notes

All three content personas apply in paid search — match to the ad group's target audience:

**Erin (Technical Founder)** — default for the on-call scheduling and small-team ad groups
- Searching for: "on call scheduling for small teams," "free on call software," "how to set up an on call rotation"
- She *is* the rotation today, and has no interest in becoming a part-time SRE. The conversion action is a free-tier signup, not a demo request.
- Copy angle: set up in one afternoon, free up to 5 responders, no card, no migration project

**Aaron (Platform Engineer)** — default for the provider, API, and technical long-tail ad groups
- Searching for: "terraform on call rotation," "incident management API," "alert deduplication," "escalation policy examples"
- He reads the provider docs before he books anything. The ad's job is to get him to the docs, not to a form — send this ad group to the documentation, and count the trial start.
- Copy angle: ownership as code, open API on every tier, declare the rotation in a pull request, no enterprise gate

**Hannah (VP Engineering)** — default for category head terms and competitor campaigns
- Searching for: "best incident management tools," "incident management platform," "{{COMPETITOR_A_LOWER}} alternative," "how do engineering teams measure on-call fairness"
- She owns the uptime number and the on-call policy, and she needs trust rather than enthusiasm. She is evaluating platforms, not impulse-buying.
- Copy angle: MTTR and MTTA, clear ownership, fairness reporting, migration without re-training

In competitive campaigns all three work — Erin for setup-speed framing, Aaron for the
migration mechanics, Hannah for the business case. Aaron is the one to lead with when the
query carries a tool name plus a technical modifier ("{{COMPETITOR_A_LOWER}} terraform,"
"{{COMPETITOR_B_LOWER}} api"): those searchers are already mid-evaluation.
